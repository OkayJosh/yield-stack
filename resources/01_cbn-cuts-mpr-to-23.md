*User prompt: how does this affects fintechs in lending*

Response: The CBN’s 350-basis-point policy rate cut (from 26.5% to 23.0%) reshapes credit economics across digital lending, B2B embedded finance, and wholesale debt capital. While headline benchmark cuts signal cheaper credit, the structural details—specifically retaining the Cash Reserve Requirement (CRR) at 45% for Deposit Money Banks—create an asymmetric playing field between digital lenders and traditional banks.

### **1\. Wholesale Cost of Debt & Commercial Paper (CP) Pricing**

> * **Compression in Wholesale Funding Yields:** Fintechs that finance their loan books via local currency debt facilities, institutional debt syndicates, or short-term Commercial Paper (CP) issuances will see their marginal cost of funds decrease as secondary treasury and prime interbank yields slide.  
> * **Corridor Floor Squeeze (-300 bps):** The Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) rate dropping to 20.0% (23%−300 bps) depresses the risk-free rate institutions can earn simply by parking excess cash at the central bank. This pushes institutional asset managers and family offices back toward private credit and high-yielding fintech debt notes.

### **2\. Net Interest Margins (NIM) & Lending Models**

The impact diverges sharply depending on whether a fintech operates on consumer nano-credit or B2B/SME trade finance:

> * **Consumer Digital Lenders (Unsecured Nano-Loans):**  
  * Typically price risk on monthly flat rates (e.g., 5% to 10% per month) rather than prime floating benchmarks.  
  * Because borrower APRs are largely inelastic to a 350 bps policy change, their lending yields remain elevated while their institutional cost of capital drops, leading to an **immediate expansion of Net Interest Margins (NIM)**.  
> * **B2B, Working Capital, & Trade Finance Lenders:**  
  * These facilities are heavily benchmarked against floating commercial rates (*MPR*\+*X*%).  
  * Prime SME borrowers will demand benchmark adjustments from \~30–32% down to 26–28%. While nominal gross yields contract, lower default risk and increased corporate drawdowns balance out unit economics.

### **3\. The CRR Liquidity Trap: Fintechs vs. Commercial Banks**

> * **The 45% CRR Barrier:** Retaining the DMB Cash Reserve Ratio at **45.0%** means traditional commercial banks still have nearly half of their customer deposits sterilized with zero yield at the CBN.  
> * **The Fintech Arbitrage:** Because commercial banks remain liquidity-constrained and cautious about expanding unsecured balance sheets, the "credit transmission gap" persists. Digital lenders with independent capital pools or Microfinance Bank (MFB) subsidiaries can capture unmet retail and SME credit demand without direct competition from conservative tier-1 bank desks.

### **4\. Underwriting, Default Rates, and Debt Serviceability**

> * **Macro Debt Serviceability:** Moderating headline inflation alongside lower debt-servicing costs improves the Debt-Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) for merchant borrowers and salary-earning consumers.  
> * **Reduction in Non-Performing Loans (NPLs):** High policy rates previously strained borrower disposable cash flows, increasing retail delinquency. Easing monetary policy reduces systemic default risk across existing floating portfolios.

### **5\. Treasury Management: Deposit Mobilization vs. Lending Allocation**

> * **Declining High-Yield Savings Appeal:** Fintechs that doubled as consumer neobanks used 16–22% APYs on "high-yield" savings features to suck in deposits, reinvesting those funds into high-yielding Treasury Bills or CBN windows.  
> * **Forced Asset Reallocation:** As government instrument yields contract, neobanks can no longer rely on passive carry trades. To maintain operational margins, platforms will be forced to aggressively allocate their deposit liabilities toward active loan origination.
